Most Nigerian small businesses do not fail because the books were wrong. They
run into trouble because nobody could say, at the end of a good month, whether
the money in the account was profit or somebody else's.

Keeping books properly is how you tell the difference. Here is what that
actually involves at a small business's size, without the jargon.

## What you are legally required to keep

Whatever your size, you need records of what came in, what went out, and what
you still owe or are owed. In practice that means five things:

- **Sales invoices** you issued, numbered in order and not skipping any
- **Purchase invoices and receipts** from your suppliers
- **Bank statements** for every account the business uses
- **Payroll records** if you have staff, including PAYE deducted
- **A record of money the business owes and is owed**

Keep them for at least six years. The tax office can go back, and the burden of
proving a figure is yours, not theirs.

## Which taxes apply at your size

This is where the 2026 rules changed things in small businesses' favour. The
thresholds now are:

| | Applies when |
| --- | --- |
| Company income tax | Turnover above ₦100 million, or fixed assets above ₦250 million |
| VAT | Turnover of ₦50 million or more |
| Withholding tax | Always, on payments you make to suppliers |
| PAYE | Always, if you pay salaries |

A company under ₦100 million in turnover with fixed assets under ₦250 million is
exempt from company income tax, capital gains tax and the development levy.
That line was ₦25 million before January 2026, so a lot of businesses that used
to pay now do not.

**Being exempt from a tax is not the same as being exempt from filing.** You
still register, you still have a TIN, and you still file your annual return.
Businesses get fined for not filing far more often than for not paying.

## The deadlines that catch people

| What | When |
| --- | --- |
| VAT return and payment | 21st of the following month |
| Withholding tax, to the federal service | 21st of the following month |
| Withholding tax, to a state service | 30th of the following month |
| PAYE remittance | 10th of the following month |
| Annual company income tax return | Six months after your year end |

The monthly ones are the dangerous ones, because they come round twelve times a
year and the penalties stack each month you miss. Our guides to
[VAT returns](/guides/vat-returns-nigeria) and
[withholding tax](/guides/withholding-tax-nigeria) go through each in detail.

## Separate the business from yourself

This is the single most useful thing a small Nigerian business can do, and it
costs nothing.

**Open a bank account in the business's name and use only that account for the
business.** Not your personal account. Not sometimes.

Everything else gets easier once you do: your bank statement becomes your
record, your profit figure means something, your accountant's bill goes down
because they are not unpicking which transfers were yours and which were the
company's, and if the tax office ever asks, you have an answer.

When you take money out, record it as what it is: salary, or drawings, or a
dividend. Each is taxed differently and "I just transferred it" is not one of
the options.

## The five mistakes that cost the most

**Treating VAT as income.** The 7.5% you added is not yours. If you spend it,
you will be short on the 21st, and the penalties for not remitting are worse
than for not filing.

**Forgetting to deduct withholding tax.** If you should have deducted it from a
supplier and did not, the tax office comes to you for it. You can rarely get it
back from the supplier months later.

**Not collecting TINs.** You need a supplier's TIN to claim input VAT on their
invoice, and without one the WHT rate you must deduct is doubled. Collect it
before you pay, not after.

**Paying from the wrong account.** Once business and personal money mix, nobody
can produce a believable set of accounts, including you.

**Leaving it all to year end.** Reconstructing twelve months of records in
January is how a small business ends up with an accountant's bill larger than
the tax it owed.

## How much bookkeeping do you actually need?

More than a notebook, less than a finance department.

At a minimum, every week: record the invoices you issued, record the bills and
receipts that came in, and match your bank account against your records. An
hour a week, done consistently, beats three days in a panic.

What makes that hour short is having everything in one place. If invoices are
in a notebook, receipts in a bag and payments only on the bank statement, each
week starts with assembling the pieces. If they go in as they happen, the week's
work is mostly reading.

## When to get an accountant

Sooner than most people do, but not for the data entry.

Use an accountant for the things that need judgement: your year-end return, a
tax question, whether you have crossed a threshold, how to take money out of the
business. Do not pay one to type in your invoices — that is the expensive way to
buy bookkeeping, and they would rather you did not either.

What they want from you is a clean set of records they can work from. Give them
that and their bill is for advice rather than for archaeology.
