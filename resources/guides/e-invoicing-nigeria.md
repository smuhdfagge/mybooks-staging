Nigeria is moving to electronic invoicing. Instead of simply issuing an invoice
to your customer, you send it to the tax authority's system first, it checks it,
and it hands back a number and a QR code. That validated invoice is the one that
counts.

This is being brought in by size of business, largest first. The question most
owners have is simply: **does this apply to me, and when?**

## When it reaches your business

The Nigeria Revenue Service set out a phased plan in February 2026. The groups
are by annual turnover:

| Your turnover | Goes live | Enforcement starts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₦5 billion and above | 1 August 2025 | Already in force |
| ₦1 billion to ₦5 billion | 1 July 2026 | Early 2027 |
| Below ₦1 billion | 1 July 2027 | Early 2028 |

Most Nigerian small and medium businesses sit in that last row. If that is you,
your date is **July 2027**, with enforcement not expected until 2028.

That is not a reason to forget about it. It is a reason to stop worrying about
it this month and deal with it properly next year.

## How it actually works

The system is the Electronic Fiscal System, which you will also see called the
**Merchant Buyer Solution (MBS)**.

In practice:

1. You raise an invoice as you do now.
2. Your software sends it to the MBS portal.
3. The portal checks the details and returns a **unique invoice number and a QR
   code**.
4. That validated invoice is the legally compliant document for the sale.

The point of it, from the government's side, is that VAT and income are reported
as they happen rather than summarised by you once a month. The point of it from
your side is that it has to happen automatically, because nobody is going to key
every invoice into a second system by hand.

## What this means for your records

Two things follow, and the second one is the one that bites.

**Your invoicing has to connect to the system.** Not a separate programme you
type invoices into again, but the software you already raise invoices in,
sending them across as you go. Businesses that buy a standalone e-invoicing tool
usually find it does not link to anything and they are doing the work twice.

**Your customers' invoices matter to you too.** Where an invoice has not been
validated, the input VAT on it can be at risk. That is the real cost of a
supplier who is not compliant: you may lose the VAT you paid them. If you are
registered for VAT, this is worth understanding now — our guide to
[VAT returns in Nigeria](/guides/vat-returns-nigeria) explains how input VAT
works.

## What to do before your date

If you are below ₦1 billion, you have until July 2027. A sensible order:

- **Check which band you are in.** It is annual turnover, so a business near
  ₦1 billion should work out which side of the line it falls.
- **Get your invoicing into one system.** Whatever happens with the details,
  invoices raised in a spreadsheet or a notebook cannot be sent anywhere
  automatically. This is the part that takes time, and it is worth doing for its
  own sake.
- **Make sure your customer records are clean.** TINs, registered names and
  addresses have to match what the tax office holds. Invoices are rejected for
  bad data more often than for anything else.
- **Ask your software provider what their plan is.** The answer tells you
  whether you will be ready or scrambling.

## What is still unclear

Some of this is still settling, and anyone who tells you otherwise is guessing:

- The penalties for non-compliance have not been set out in figures. What has
  been said is that the risks include penalties and losing input VAT claims.
- The detail of how smaller businesses will connect is still being worked
  through; the phased plan deliberately leaves the smallest group until last.
- Dates have moved before. Large taxpayer onboarding was extended once already.

Check the position again closer to your date rather than acting on what was true
in 2026.
